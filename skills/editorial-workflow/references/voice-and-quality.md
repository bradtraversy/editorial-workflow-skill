# Voice and Quality Guide

Ground the article in the author's real language, experience, and point of view.

## Voice modes

Choose one primary mode:

| Mode | Best for | Main characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion | Arguments, reactions, changing views | Direct, skeptical, nuanced, conversational |
| Reflective | Personal essays, project meaning, changes in direction | Honest, specific, comfortable with uncertainty |
| Teaching | Tutorials, concepts, workflow explanations | Practical, sequential, audience-aware, concrete |
| Conversational | Informal posts, notes, current observations | Immediate, plainspoken, lightly structured |

Borrow from a secondary mode only when the material genuinely requires it.

## Evidence priority

Use voice evidence in this order:

1. Clearly author-written material matching the selected mode.
2. The author's exact language in the current conversation.
3. Approved notes, captures, or transcripts with preserved exact phrases.
4. Final drafts the author substantially edited.
5. Summaries for facts and decisions only.

Do not learn voice from unedited AI drafts, generated ideas, digests, research summaries, or uncertain-authorship files.

Read two to four relevant sources. Record observations and short phrases in the voice packet. Do not paste large portions of prior work.

## Voice target

Aim for writing that is:

- Direct and conversational
- Experienced without sounding performative
- Specific about what happened and what changed
- Honest about tradeoffs and unresolved questions
- Technical when detail matters and plain when it does not
- Confident where evidence is strong
- Varied naturally in rhythm and paragraph length

Correct typing errors and transcription artifacts. The author's voice is not a collection of mistakes.

## Patterns to avoid

- Generic hooks about a rapidly changing world
- Stock phrases such as `let's dive in`, `game changer`, and `unlock`
- Fake epiphanies or emotions
- Excessive headings, tidy frameworks, or forced life lessons
- Repetitive one-sentence paragraphs for artificial drama
- Repeated contrast formulas
- Unnecessary rhetorical questions
- Marketing language and keyword padding
- Claims that every problem was solved
- Conclusions that repeat the introduction

## Evidence rules

- Separate exact quotations, verified facts, firsthand accounts, and interpretation.
- Paraphrase when exact wording is unavailable.
- Generalize sensitive details only when the truth remains intact.
- Remove or flag unverified numbers, dates, results, and causal claims.
- Keep private evidence paths out of public copy.

## Author-specific test

For every major section ask:

1. Could another author publish this unchanged?
2. Which sourced detail makes it specific?
3. Is the personal element attributable and public-safe?
4. Did editing remove an honest complication?

When author-specific material is missing, mark `[AUTHOR INPUT NEEDED]`. Never invent it.

## Final pass

Ask:

1. Is the thesis clear without a mechanical announcement?
2. Does every section earn its place?
3. Which details make the experience specific?
4. Is any sentence polished but empty?
5. Is any claim stronger than its source?
6. Could the opening start later?
7. Could the ending stop earlier?
