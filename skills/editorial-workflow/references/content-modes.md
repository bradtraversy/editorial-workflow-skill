# Content Modes and Research Depth

Route a writing request before research begins.

## Search guide

Choose when the reader arrives with a discoverable question, task, or problem.

Prioritize search intent, primary documentation, current authoritative explanations, recurring reader questions, coverage gaps, demonstrations, examples, and useful assets.

Avoid copying ranking-page structure, treating competitor claims as facts, padding word count, or forcing keywords into the draft.

## Project story

Choose when the core value is why something was built, how decisions changed, or what happened while shipping it.

Prioritize project records, implementation evidence, the original problem, constraints, tradeoffs, failed approaches, result, current limits, and the author's exact perspective.

Avoid turning the story into a generic tutorial, inventing motivations, or hiding complications.

## Opinion

Choose when the piece argues a position, reacts to a change, or explains a change of mind.

Prioritize the author's actual position, strong evidence, serious counterarguments, definitions, firsthand experience, and appropriate uncertainty.

Avoid manufactured outrage, weak straw arguments, and research that erases the author's point of view.

## Personal essay

Choose when lived experience and its meaning are the center of the piece.

Prioritize the author's firsthand account, timeline, concrete details, exact approved language, public-safety boundaries, and only the outside context needed.

Avoid searching private material without approval, assuming restricted writing is public-safe, or forcing a motivational lesson.

## Comparison

Choose when the reader needs a decision between products, tools, approaches, or technologies.

Prioritize clear criteria, hands-on testing when possible, official capabilities, current pricing and availability, best fit by context, and important unknowns.

Avoid declaring one universal winner, comparing marketing pages without testing, or hiding firsthand-use and commercial limitations.

## Timely analysis

Choose when explaining a recent announcement, release, policy, or ecosystem change.

Prioritize the primary announcement, current official documentation, the previous state, independent confirmation, practical effect, and open questions.

Avoid treating speculation as confirmed impact, using stale knowledge, or publishing urgency without verification.

## Case study

Choose when demonstrating a process and observable result.

Prioritize the initial state, goal, constraints, intervention, evidence of result, confounding factors, and limits on generalization.

Avoid unsupported causation, hidden failed steps, and vanity measurements without reader value.

## Hybrid decisions

Use a hybrid only when two modes are essential. Name one primary and one secondary mode. The primary mode controls structure. Do not use hybrid to avoid a decision.

## Research depth

### Quick

Use for narrow, low-risk, short, or timely work with strong sources already available.

Minimum:

- Brief or documented assumptions
- Relevant source check
- Lightweight outline
- Draft
- One independent review plus fact verification

### Standard

Use for normal public work where multiple source types, voice, or factual quality matter.

Minimum:

- Approved brief
- Relevant research tracks
- Source ledger and voice packet
- Angle and outline approval
- Draft
- Independent fact, reader, and voice reviews
- Technical, argument, or search review when applicable
- Revision and package

### Cornerstone

Use for authoritative, competitive, technical, consequential, or commercially important work.

Minimum:

- Deep research plan
- Multiple independent research tracks
- Claim and contradiction ledger
- Original evidence when possible
- Full review board
- Visual and enrichment plan
- Performance and refresh plan

## Routing questions

Ask only questions that change execution:

1. What should the reader understand, decide, or do?
2. Why is the author the right person to write this?
3. Is search discovery important, secondary, or irrelevant?
4. Where will the canonical article live?
5. What would make the piece different from existing coverage?
6. What is the cost of getting a claim wrong?
7. Is firsthand evidence required before drafting?
